Before You Start

  • Set a timer. Five to 15 minutes on one shelf or drawer is enough for a session.
  • Grab four boxes: donate, recycle, trash and put-away, so nothing gets sorted twice.
  • Work top to bottom in each room, and finish one spot before you start the next.
  • Get it out the same day. Put donations in the car and recycling by the door before you stop.

The Declutter Checklist, Room by Room

Each line ends with where the item goes. Rules for recycling and hazardous waste vary by town, so check your local guidance for the drop-off points near you.

Kitchen and pantry

  • Expired or stale pantry food (check before you toss) (trash)
  • Freezer food with freezer burn or no label (trash)
  • Duplicate utensils and gadgets you have not used in a year (donate)
  • Extra mugs and water bottles that do not fit one shelf (donate)
  • Food containers with no matching lid (recycle)
  • Old sponges and worn dish towels (trash)
  • Takeout menus, sauce packets, rubber bands (trash)

Bathroom and medicine cabinet

  • Expired or unused medicines (take-back)
  • Mascara older than 3 months (trash)
  • Eye makeup you used during an eye infection (trash)
  • Sunscreen past its date, or over 3 years old (trash)
  • Samples and hotel toiletries you will not use (donate)
  • Old towels and bath mats (donate)
  • Empty bottles and tubes (recycle)

Bedroom and closet

  • Clothes that do not fit or you have not worn in a year (donate)
  • Shoes and bags in good shape you no longer use (donate)
  • Worn-out clothes, odd socks, stretched underwear (recycle)
  • Bedding sets beyond two per bed (donate)
  • Broken jewelry and tangled chains (trash)
  • Wire hangers and extra plastic hangers (recycle)

Living room and electronics

  • Old phones and tablets (back up, remove SIM, reset) (recycle)
  • Chargers and cables for devices you no longer own (recycle)
  • Used batteries (tape lithium-ion terminals) (hazardous)
  • Books, DVDs and games you will not use again (donate)
  • Old magazines and catalogs (recycle)
  • Extra throw pillows and blankets (donate)

Home office and paperwork

  • Bank statements, bills and card offers you no longer need (shred)
  • Tax records past the IRS keep period (shred)
  • Manuals you can find online (recycle)
  • Warranties for things you no longer own (shred)
  • Dried-out pens and used notebooks (trash)
  • Home purchase and improvement records (keep)

Kids' rooms and baby gear

  • Outgrown clothes and shoes (donate)
  • Toys and puzzles with missing pieces (trash)
  • Drop-side cribs or cribs made before June 28, 2011 (trash)
  • Car seats past their date or after a moderate crash (trash)
  • Outgrown baby gear (check for recalls first) (donate)
  • School papers and art: keep a few favorites (keep)

Garage, laundry and cleaning shelf

  • Old paint, solvents, pesticides and motor oil (hazardous)
  • Cleaners and drain openers you no longer use (hazardous)
  • Broken tools and duplicates (recycle)
  • Outgrown bikes and sports gear (donate)
  • Smoke alarms 10 years old or older (replace)
  • Dried-out or half-used laundry products (trash)

Where It Goes: The Rules Worth Knowing

Medicines: take-back first

The FDA says a drug take-back program is the best way to dispose of most types of expired, unwanted or unused medicines. The DEA counts more than 15,300 pharmacies, hospitals, and businesses that take them year-round, as well as twice-yearly National Prescription Drug Take Back Days. If there is no take-back option, the FDA says to mix medicines with something undesirable such as used coffee grounds, dirt or cat litter, seal it in a bag or container, put it in the trash and scratch your details off the packaging. Only flush medicines that are on the FDA flush list.

Makeup, sunscreen and food

Cosmetics don't need an expiration date by law, but the FDA notes that some industry experts recommend replacing mascara 3 months after purchase, and it says to throw out eye makeup you were using when you had an eye infection. Sunscreen is required to keep its original strength for at least three years, according to Mayo Clinic, which says to throw out any that is past its date. In the pantry, a "Best if Used By" date indicates when a product will be of best flavor or quality, not when it becomes unsafe, so check the food itself before you throw it away.

Electronics and batteries

The EPA says lithium-ion batteries should NOT go in household garbage or recycling bins, because they can start fires. Take them to a battery drop-off or household hazardous waste site, with the terminals taped or each battery in its own bag. Before you recycle or donate a phone or laptop, the EPA says to delete all personal information, and the FTC's three steps for a phone are to back it up, remove the SIM card and restore or reset it.

Paint, chemicals and cleaners

Old paint, solvents, pesticides, motor oil and drain openers are household hazardous waste. The EPA recommends that you not pour them down the drain, on the ground or into storm sewers, and that you use HHW collection programs rather than discarding the HHW in the trash. Many towns run collection days or a permanent drop-off site.

Clothes and textiles

Donate what is clean and wearable, and look for a textile recycling drop-off for the rest. It adds up: the EPA says landfills received 11.3 million tons of textiles in 2018.

Cribs and car seats: don't pass them on

The CPSC says you should not resell, donate or give away a crib that does not meet the federal standard that took effect on June 28, 2011, which rules out drop-side cribs; take an old crib apart before you throw it away. For car seats, NHTSA recommends that car seats be replaced following a moderate or severe crash, and many seats also have an expiration date printed on the seat or in the manual. If you are not sure of a seat's history, don't donate it.

What Not to Throw Out

Paperwork is where decluttering can go wrong. The IRS says to keep records for 3 years in most cases, but 6 years if you didn't report income you should have, 7 years if you claimed a loss from worthless securities or a bad debt, and indefinitely if you didn't file a return. Keep records for a property, such as your purchase and home improvement receipts, until the limitations period ends for the year you sell it. When papers do go, the FTC advises you to shred all documents that show personal, financial, and medical information.

While you are in each room, check the smoke alarms too. The U.S. Fire Administration says to replace smoke alarms when they are 10 years old.
