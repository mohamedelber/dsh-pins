To store vinyl records, stand them upright in their sleeves, never stacked flat, with enough support that they don't lean. Keep them somewhere steady, cool and dark: the Library of Congress recommends 65 to 70°F and 45 to 50% relative humidity for medium-term storage. Swap old paper inner sleeves for polyethylene ones and keep records out of the attic, the garage and the sun.

That's the short version. The seven steps below cover what to do before a record goes on the shelf, how to choose furniture that can take the weight, and where in the house to put it.

Step 1: Clean Each Record Before You Store It

Dust that goes onto the shelf in the grooves stays there. Gaylord Archival, an archival storage supplier, says to clean a record both before and after playback. For routine dust, a dry carbon fiber brush is the usual tool; Audio-Technica says its anti-static brush is designed for dry cleaning records and shouldn't be used with liquid. Don't overdo it: the Canadian Conservation Institute warns that frequent or improper cleaning may cause more problems. Hold records by the edge and the label only, as the Library of Congress advises; Gaylord notes that oils from your hands can transfer into the grooves and attract dust.

Step 2: Replace Paper Inner Sleeves

Plain paper sleeves can scuff the surface and shed fibers. The Library of Congress says most sleeves should be replaced with a high density polyethylene sleeve, and the Canadian Conservation Institute recommends soft polyethylene sleeves inside the original jacket, while avoiding PVC and acidic cardboard sleeves. If a sleeve feels sticky or tacky, swap it. Want to keep a printed original inner sleeve? Thin poly sleeves can go inside it, so you keep the artwork and still protect the disc.

Step 3: Take Off the Shrink Wrap

Many collectors leave new records in their shrink wrap, but it is better to remove it. Gaylord says the wrap can continue to shrink, which can damage the record, and a conservator answering on the American Institute for Conservation's Connecting to Collections Care forum notes that tight plastic wrapping may cause warping. If you want to protect the jacket, slide it into a loose clear outer sleeve instead.

Step 4: Stand Records Upright, Never Flat

Every archive cited here agrees on this one. The Library of Congress says to keep discs standing upright, on edge and not to lay any recording flat, and the National Archives says do not stack discs on top of one another. Gaylord explains why: to minimize warping, records should be stored vertically. Records that lean can warp too. The National Archives of Australia says boxes or dividers should give support so that the discs do not lean over. Fill a part-empty shelf or crate with a bookend or a block so the last few stay straight.

Step 5: Add Dividers and Sort by Size

For long runs of records, the Library of Congress recommends full-height and full-depth dividers spaced 4 to 6 inches apart, and warns that shorter dividers may contribute to warping. It also says mixing discs of different diameters on one shelf can warp them, so keep 7-inch singles, 10-inch records and 12-inch LPs in separate sections. Gaylord agrees: store records of the same size together. Pack them snugly but not crammed, so you can pull one out without forcing it.

Step 6: Use Furniture That Can Take the Weight

Records are heavy. The Library of Congress says LPs average between 35 and 45 pounds per shelf-foot, and that the weight concentrates along the middle of a shelf, which is where shelves sag. A standard LP weighs about 140 grams according to vinyl manufacturer Disc Makers, and 180-gram pressings are heavier still. That matters with cube shelving. IKEA's page for a two-cube KALLAX shelf unit lists Max load/shelf 29 lb, while a full 13-inch cube of LPs would weigh roughly 38 to 49 pounds by our estimate. Many collectors use KALLAX anyway, but check the rated load, don't cram every cube with heavy pressings, and anchor the unit to the wall: IKEA's product page warns of a tipping hazard and says it must be securely anchored.

Check the depth too. A 12-inch LP jacket is a little over 12 inches square, a bit more in an outer sleeve, so as a rule of thumb look for about 13 inches of clear depth and height. IKEA's small EKET cabinet, for example, is only 9 7/8" deep, so LPs would stick out. Short on space? A sturdy wooden crate on a sideboard holds a few dozen records near the turntable, and the rest can go in a closet or spare room, which the National Archives says are often good choices. Step 7: Pick a Cool, Dry, Dark Spot

Steady conditions matter as much as good shelving. The Canadian Conservation Institute says temperature swings are a problem for all media, but more so with vinyl discs. The National Archives suggests keeping audio recordings within the ranges of 55-70 degrees Fahrenheit and 30-55% relative humidity, and the Library of Congress figure of 65 to 70°F and 45 to 50% humidity is close to ordinary room conditions, so a living room or bedroom usually works; a cheap hygrometer will show whether your humidity stays in range.

  • Avoid the attic and garage. The National Archives says to avoid an attic or garage because their climate is unregulated.
  • Basements are a maybe. The National Archives says basements are generally darker, cooler and more stable, but humidity levels can be high unless regulated by air conditioning or a dehumidifier. Choose another floor if yours ever floods, and keep records off the floor wherever they are.
  • Keep out of the sun and away from heat. The National Archives of Australia warns that vinyl discs will readily buckle if exposed to heat or sunlight, so don't store them by a radiator, a heating vent or a sunny window, or leave them in a parked car.
  • Watch the humidity. The Canadian Conservation Institute warns that high relative humidity raises the risk of mold for all recording media.

78s and Singles Need Extra Care

Most old 78 rpm records are made of shellac, not vinyl. The University of Illinois preservation program describes them as rigid and brittle, and the Library of Congress notes 78s are even heavier than LPs. Give them their own shelf, upright with good support, and check that any cleaning fluid is safe for shellac: Audio-Technica says its AT6012 record care kit is designed specifically for LP and EP vinyl and should not be used on shellac 78s. Keep 7-inch singles in a box or crate sized for them, rather than lost between LPs.

Storing Vinyl: Quick Answers

Is it bad to store vinyl records at an angle?

Yes, if they lean for long. Records that sit at an angle put uneven pressure on the discs, and archives such as the National Archives of Australia recommend dividers or boxes that keep them from leaning. If a crate or shelf is only part full, fill the gap with a bookend or block so the records stand straight.

Should you keep the plastic on vinyl records?

Take off the original shrink wrap, because it can keep shrinking and warp the record. To protect the jacket, use a loose outer sleeve instead.
