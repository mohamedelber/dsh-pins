Before You Shop

  • Measure first. Doorways, stairs and the bedroom, before you buy a bed or a sofa.
  • Day 1 items first. Your first night needs light, a bed, a towel, toilet paper and something to eat off.
  • Borrow or buy used for the Later list, but inspect secondhand furniture first: the EPA says do not bring second-hand furniture into your home until you have thoroughly inspected and cleaned it for bed bugs.
  • Keep receipts and photos of what you buy; the Texas Department of Insurance suggests you fill out a home inventory so you know what your belongings are worth if you need to claim.

Why These Items Are on the List

Renters insurance and the move-in inspection

The NAIC, the US association of state insurance regulators, warns that your landlord's insurance doesn't cover your personal property. The Insurance Information Institute puts the national average premium at $171 a year in 2022 (about $14 a month), and the Texas Department of Insurance says about $20 a month is typical there. No state law requires it, the Texas Department of Insurance says, but it notes that some landlords might require you to have a renters policy, so check your lease.

Your security deposit depends on proving what was already wrong. A legal assistance attorney at Fort Rucker's Office of the Staff Judge Advocate, writing on Army.mil, advises you to ask your landlord for a move-in inspection sheet, note any and every small scratch or stain and take photos when you move in and out. Deposit deadlines vary by state: New York requires landlords to return it within 14 days and Texas within 30 days once you give a forwarding address, minus any deductions. Check your own state's rules.

Utilities, mail and the budget

The University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign's Off-Campus Community Living office suggests calling utility companies at least one month prior to your move-in date. The USPS lets you file a change of address 90 days before the effective date of your move, with a $1.25 authentication fee online. On budget, HUD's research office notes that households that spend more than 30 percent of income on housing costs are considered cost burdened, which is why keeping rent plus utilities at or under about 30% of your gross income is the usual rule of thumb. If your building was built before 1978, federal rules usually require your landlord to give you the EPA lead hazard pamphlet and disclose any known information about lead-based paint before you sign (studios, leases of 100 days or less and certified lead-free units are exempt).

Smoke alarms, CO alarms and a fire extinguisher

On your first day, test every smoke alarm. The US Fire Administration says to put smoke alarms inside and outside each bedroom and on every level, to test smoke alarms every month and that a dead or missing battery is the same as having no alarm. In some states the landlord must provide them; the Texas Attorney General notes that the landlord must also provide smoke detectors. Carbon monoxide alarms belong outside each separate sleeping area and on every level, and the USFA, citing CPSC data, says more than 150 people in the United States die every year from accidental non-fire CO poisoning.

For a fire extinguisher, the USFA notes that most home improvement stores carry multipurpose fire extinguishers that cover Class A through Class C, which work on most home fires. Learn the PASS steps: pull the pin, aim low at the base of the fire, squeeze the lever slowly and sweep from side to side. Only fight a fire that is small and contained, after everyone is alerted and 911 has been called, and when you have a clear way out; otherwise get out and call 911. While you are at it, plan 2 ways out of every room.

Anchors, kitchen safety and cleaning

The CPSC's Anchor It campaign says to anchor TVs and furniture, such as bookcases and dressers, and reports that 17,800 people annually are injured in tip-overs; most anti-tip kits cost less than $20 and take under 20 minutes to install. Ask your landlord before drilling. In the kitchen, the FDA says to keep the fridge at 40 °F or below; the freezer should be at 0 °F, which is why a fridge thermometer is on the list, and FoodSafety.gov's chart puts poultry at 165°F. Use separate cutting boards for raw meat and produce. When you clean, Poison Control warns that ammonia should never be mixed with chlorine or bleach products, and the CDC says to use an EPA-registered disinfecting product where you need to disinfect.

The right size bed

A mattress is the biggest Day 1 purchase, so measure the room first. The Sleep Foundation lists a full at 54” wide, 75” long and a queen at 60 by 80 inches, and suggests leaving about 24 inches of space around each side of your bed. Buy a mattress encasement too; the EPA lists encasements on mattress and box spring among its bed bug control steps.
