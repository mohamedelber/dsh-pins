A dresser is wide and low, with two or three columns of drawers and room for a mirror on top; a chest of drawers is tall and narrow, with one stacked column. Pick a dresser if you have a long wall and want a surface; pick a chest if floor space is tight. In a matched IKEA pair with six drawers each, the tall chest uses half the floor for about 81% of the storage, and costs $20 less. Whichever you choose, anchor it to the wall: the CPSC says a chest, bureau or dresser was involved in 79 out of 217 tip-over deaths it recorded from 2013 to mid-2023 (36 percent).

The Dresser: Best For, Avoid If

Merriam-Webster defines a dresser as a chest of drawers or bureau with a mirror, and Ashley says its drawers are organized horizontally into two or three columns. The wide top is the main reason to buy one: it holds a lamp, a tray and a mirror, and at 30 to 36 inches it sits at a comfortable height to stand at. Lowe's adds that a low piece suits a room with a low ceiling, because a dresser will help create the illusion of a larger room.

  • Best for: a primary bedroom with a long, unbroken wall; couples who want one drawer column each; anyone who wants a dressing surface with a mirror.
  • Avoid if: the only free wall is under 5 feet, the room is narrow (a 20-inch-deep dresser plus 36 inches to open it needs about 4 feet 8 inches from the wall), or you planned to put a TV on top (see the safety section).

The Chest of Drawers: Best For, Avoid If

A chest stacks its drawers in one column. Lowe's puts a typical chest between 44 and 60 inches tall and between 24 and 38 inches wide, and calls chests perfect for corners and small rooms. Very narrow ones exist: IKEA's STORKLINTA 5-drawer is 15 3/4 inches wide, small enough for the gap beside a door or window. A chest also fits inside many closets; Apartment Therapy notes that closets are usually about 24 inches deep, while dressers usually measure less than 20; both STORKLINTA units are 18 7/8.

  • Best for: small bedrooms, a narrow wall between a window and a door, a closet, kids' rooms, and a second storage piece next to a dresser.
  • Avoid if: you want a surface to get ready at, or the top drawers would sit above a short person's eye level. Typical chests reach up to 60 inches.

Bureau, Highboy, Lowboy, Tallboy: What the Names Mean

The names overlap, and US and UK English use them differently. In North American English a bureau is simply a chest of drawers, but in British English it is a desk with drawers. Merriam-Webster defines a highboy as a tall chest of drawers with a legged base; Britannica notes that double chests, or chest-on-chests, were once known as tallboys in England and highboys in America. A lowboy is a low chest of drawers on short legs. And a British "dresser" is something else again: a large piece of wooden furniture with shelves in the top part and cupboards below, traditionally used for dishes. Retailers blur the terms too: IKEA US titles both STORKLINTA 6-drawer units, the wide one and the 47-inch-tall single column, "dresser", yet its product copy calls each one a "chest of drawers", so go by the measurements.

Whichever You Pick, Anchor It

Tall or wide, drawer units can tip when a child climbs the open drawers. The CPSC recorded 217 reports of product instability or tip-over-related fatalities from January 2013 to July 2023; 71% of the victims were children under 18, and a chest, bureau or dresser was involved in 79 of the deaths. The agency says a child is treated in an emergency department every 53 minutes for a tip-over injury.

Clothing storage units manufactured after September 1, 2023 must meet a federal stability standard under the STURDY Act. It covers units 27 in. (686 mm) or greater in height that weigh 30 pounds or more and hold at least 3.2 cubic feet, so both of our STORKLINTA examples are covered. Covered units must pass stability tests and must also include an anti-tip device. Older furniture is not covered, so anchor it too.

  • Use the kit. The CPSC says to anchor TVs and furniture, such as bookcases and dressers, securely to the wall. It adds that most kits cost less than $20.
  • Load it low. CPSC advice: store heavier items on lower shelves, or in lower drawers.
  • No TV on top. Consumer Reports' advice is to avoid placing TVs on dressers and chests; use a TV stand or wall mount instead.
  • Keep toys and remotes off the top, so children are not tempted to climb.
