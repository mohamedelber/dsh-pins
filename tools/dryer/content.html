A condenser is cheaper to buy and dries faster, but from 19 January 2027 new condenser and vented dryers can no longer be placed on the market in Great Britain, so the choice is closing anyway.

Here is the short verdict:

  • Pick a heat pump dryer if you dry more than once or twice a week, if the dryer lives in a cupboard or small flat, or if you want it to last into the 2030s.
  • Pick a condenser dryer only if you dry rarely, need the shortest cycles and can find one in stock before shops sell out.
  • Pick a vented dryer only if it can sit against an outside wall or window and you use it occasionally: it is cheap, but it pushes hot, damp air outside through a hose.

Why New Condenser and Vented Dryers Are Leaving UK Shops

New ecodesign rules for Great Britain (S.I. 2026/318) set an energy limit that only heat pump models meet. From 19 January 2027, new vented, condenser and gas-fired tumble dryers will no longer be allowed onto the GB market, according to the retail trade body RETRA. The government’s own impact work says heat pumps were already 74% of models sold, with condensers about 20% and vented about 5%.

  • You do not have to replace a working dryer. Existing machines can be used, repaired and sold second-hand.
  • Shops can sell the stock they already hold. Which? says older models placed on the market before the deadline stay on sale until stocks run out.
  • Washer-dryers are not affected by these tumble dryer rules, according to Which?.
  • Northern Ireland follows the EU rules, which set the same energy limit from 1 July 2025.

Expect confusion over labels. A new A to G label, showing kWh per 100 cycles, replaces the old A+++ scale. Some retailers already show heat pump dryers with new-scale letters such as B, C or E, which are not comparable with an old-scale B condenser. Compare the kWh figures, not the letters.

Heat Pump Dryer: Best For and Avoid If

A heat pump dryer reuses its warm air through a closed loop, so it dries at a lower temperature: about 50°C instead of the 70–75°C of a standard dryer, according to Currys. That is gentler on fabrics and the reason it uses roughly half the energy.

  • Best for: families, anyone drying three or more loads a week, flats with no outside wall, cupboards and utility rooms where you want less heat.
  • Avoid if: you need fast turnarounds. Beko quotes about 142 minutes for an 8 kg condenser cotton cycle against roughly 197 to 209 minutes for its heat pumps, and Which? found some heat pumps take almost twice as long.
  • Watch the room temperature. Beko says its dryers should not be placed where the temperature drops below 5°C, and Which? notes most brands set a minimum of 5°C to 10°C. For a cold garage, look for models designed for 0°C, such as Hotpoint ColdGuard or Beko and Blomberg DryGuard.

Heat pump dryers also need a little more care: as well as the door filter after every load, Beko advises cleaning the filters and the evaporator regularly. Some models, such as the Bosch WQG24502GB, have a self-cleaning condenser.

Condenser Dryer: Best For and Avoid If

A condenser dryer heats air with an element, then cools it to turn the moisture into water, which collects in a tank or runs away through a drain hose. No vent is needed, which is why it became the standard choice for British flats and utility cupboards.

  • Best for: occasional use (one load a week or less), when the lower price matters more than the running cost, and when you want a cycle well under two and a half hours.
  • Avoid if: you dry most days. At seven loads a week the Beko condenser costs about £336 a year, against £141 for the Hisense heat pump.
  • Expect some warm, damp air. Beko’s condenser fiche lists a condensation efficiency of 81%, so part of the water still leaves as humid air. Empty the tank after every cycle, and clean the condenser unit: Hotpoint’s manual says monthly.

Vented Dryer: Best For and Avoid If

A vented dryer blows hot, moist air straight outside through a flexible hose, so it adds the least damp to the room but must stand by an outside wall or window. Hotpoint’s installation guide says the hose should not exceed 2.4 metres, and it must not go into an extractor fan duct or a flue for a gas or other fuel-burning appliance.

  • Best for: a garage or utility room with a wall vent already in place, and light use.
  • Avoid if: the dryer would sit in an inside cupboard, or if you would have to push the hose through an open window in winter.
  • Price and choice: very few vented models are still on sale; the Logik LVD7W18 at £229 was the only one on Currys’ vented page when we checked.

Fitting a Dryer in a Small Utility Room or Cupboard

Space, not just running cost, often decides which dryer works. Heat pump and condenser dryers can go in a cupboard as long as there’s adequate space and airflow, says Beko. Hoover’s condenser manual asks for a gap of at least 12 mm around the machine and says it must not be installed behind a lockable door. Miele’s heat pump manual adds that the cool air intake at the front must not be blocked or covered.

  • Check the depth. Many 8 kg heat pumps are only 54.5 to 55 cm deep, while some 9 kg models are about 61 cm, plus the door. Our washer and dryer space calculator checks whether two machines fit your gap.
  • Stack with the right kit. The dryer always goes on top. Beko’s manual says a washing machine cannot be placed on top of the drying machine and gives minimum washer depths for each dryer depth.
  • Use the drain hose when the tank would be awkward to reach, for example in a stack or a tall cupboard: Hoover’s manual says the water can be discharged directly to a waste water drain pipe.
  • Let the damp out. Beko advises a room with a window or an extractor fan for heat pump dryers, and AEG suggests a room larger than roughly 10-12 m2. In a small cupboard, add vents or leave the door open while it runs.

Safety and Care for Any Tumble Dryer

Whichever type you choose, the fire services give the same advice.
