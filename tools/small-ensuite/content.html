The best small ensuite ideas make a tight room feel bigger by keeping the floor clear and the sightlines open: a frameless glass shower, a wall-hung vanity and toilet, a recessed niche instead of a shower caddy, and a cavity sliding door so no floor is lost to a door swing. Most Australian ensuites are between 3 and 5 m², and architect Dominic Bagnato, writing for Houzz, says a shower, basin and toilet need a minimum floor area of 3 square metres. Below are 10 ideas grouped by layout, shower, storage and finishes, with the NCC rules and costs that apply in Australia.

A Small Ensuite, Measured

Our example is 1.8 x 2.0 m, or 3.6 m², the size Blueleaf gives for a compact ensuite with a toilet, vanity and 900 x 900 mm shower. Two NCC rules shape every small layout. If a toilet is in a fully enclosed room, its door has to slide, open outwards or be readily removable from outside unless there is a clear space of at least 1.2 m between the pan and the doorway, which is why cavity sliders are so common in ensuites. And in the shower, walls must be waterproofed not less than 1800 mm above the floor substrate, with the whole shower floor waterproof.

Layout

1. Put Everything on One Wall

Moving plumbing adds cost. Laura Merlin of Origin Bathrooms told Houzz that placing the basin, toilet and shower along one wall keeps a small room open, and interior designer Kia Howat told Better Homes and Gardens that maintaining the placement of your existing toilet, shower and vanity waste locations saves money in a renovation. Bagnato adds that if the room is narrow (1 metre or less), a shower is a better choice than a bath.

2. A Cavity Sliding Door

A hinged door needs clear floor to swing into, which a small ensuite rarely has. One Australian supplier sells cavity slider units in widths from 620 to 920 mm, such as a 720 x 90 mm cavity sliding door unit, and the door slides into the wall. It also solves the NCC toilet-door rule above. Plan it before the walls go up: if you want a vanity, mirror or towel rail on the wall over the pocket, ask for extra noggings to be built into the cavity unit for fixing.

Works best: ensuites opening off a bedroom with a clear wall beside the doorway. Skip it: if the only wall beside the door holds plumbing or switches.

3. Toilet Out of the Line of Sight

Sophie Bowers, managing director of Strutt Studios, told Better Homes and Gardens that by placing a toilet near the doorway, your focus will be drawn to the more decorative elements such as the basin and vanity. Designer Kate Walker told RACV that entering front-on to your vanity creates more circulation space. If you can, put the toilet behind the door's line of sight and the vanity or shower opposite the entry.

The Shower

4. Frameless Glass and One Floor Tile

Bunnings' bathroom buyer Dan Gibney warns that doors and frames on showers in small spaces can create a closed-in feel, and RACV Trades' Andy Anderson says a clear glass frame allows you to take the whole room in. Run the same floor tile into the shower so the room reads as one space. If you go without a screen, the NCC requires a waterstop at a minimum horizontal distance of 1500 mm from the shower rose. Blueleaf calls 900 x 900 mm the standard shower size in Australia.

5. A Recessed Niche, Not a Caddy

Architect Kirsten Johnstone told Houzz to include a shower ledge or recessed alcove. Tile Cloud suggests the base of a niche sits 900mm to 1100mm from FFL (finished floor level), and Blueleaf puts the inside height at 150 to 300 mm. A niche has to fit within the wall framing or a built-out nib wall, and it must be waterproofed with the rest of the shower, so plan it with your builder before framing.

6. A Water-Efficient Showerhead

The government's WELS scheme says that for a family of four, a 3-star shower that flows at 9L/min will save 70kL and $210 a year compared with an old 15 L/min head (at 2014–15 water prices). Under the national WELS standard, a showerhead with a flow rate of not more than 7.5 litres per minute is rated WELS 4 stars. A rail shower with a hand-held head, as in our photo, also makes cleaning the glass easier.

Storage

7. A Wall-Hung Vanity With Drawers

Johnstone's advice is to wall-mount your vanity and toilet pan, so you can see and clean the floor underneath. For depth, Blueleaf gives ensuite: 600–900mm wide, 460–500mm deep, and a slimline vanity under 460 mm suits narrow rooms. Choose drawers over doors: Bunnings' ensuite guide notes that drawers, which hold up to 30 per cent more than cupboards, are the better pick for storage. An LED strip under the vanity makes a good night light.

8. A Shaving Cabinet and an In-Wall Cistern

Bowers says mirrored shaving cabinets are an excellent solution because they maximise light and add storage without cluttering the room. Three Birds Renovations chose an in-wall cistern to save space and showcase more of the tiles in a client's small ensuite, and Bunnings' Gibney suggests you opt for a wall-hung vanity and a concealed cistern. A concealed cistern sits in a framed wall, and you service it by taking off the flush plate.

Finishes

9. Large Tiles, Matching Grout and Light Colours

Christie Wood of Beaumont Tiles told Houzz that slab tiles of 800x1,600 millimetres and above are a brilliant choice for walls, and that she always recommends matching the grout colour to the tile unless you want to highlight the tile layout. Bunnings suggests large tiles in cool colours to bounce light around, and Ari Zorlu of Paco Jaanson says opt for neutrals for longevity. In a small family bathroom, Kate Bath of Blue Tea Kitchens and Bathrooms used wall tiles sparingly, to make the room feel larger.

10. A Skylight and a Proper Exhaust Fan

Many ensuites have no window. Anderson says a skylight is a fairly inexpensive way to attract natural light, and Johnstone prefers to locate skylights to capture southern light where possible. You still need extraction: the NCC calls for 25 L/s for a bathroom, discharged to outdoor air, and since NCC 2022 a fan in a bathroom that isn't naturally ventilated must continue to operate for 10 minutes after the light goes off. A heated towel rail helps towels dry; RACV puts a 200 W rail running all day at around $1.68 a day at 35c/kWh.

Before You Renovate

  • Use licensed trades. Victoria's Building and Plumbing Commission (formerly the VBA) says to engage a licensed or registered plumber for plumbing work, and Ausgrid warns that DIY electrical is not only dangerous, it's illegal. Electrical zones around the shower and basin limit where switches and power points can go.
  • Get waterproofing right. In NSW you need a contractor licence for waterproofing work valued at more than $5,000 in labour and materials, including GST, and in Queensland the QBCC says bathroom home warranty claims finalised in 2024–25 averaged almost $25,000 in rectification costs.
  • Budget. One Sydney cost guide puts ensuite renovations at $17,250–$34,500 with budget to mid-range fittings in 2026, and $40,250 to $63,250 for premium jobs. Adding a brand-new ensuite costs more, with plumbing rough-in alone at $2,300 to $5,750, and the same guide says keeping the existing layout avoids new plumbing and re-waterproofing, the biggest avoidable cost.

Mix and Match

If your budget covers only three of these small ensuite ideas, choose a frameless screen with one floor tile throughout, a wall-hung vanity with drawers, and a cavity slider. Together they free up the most floor and light, and none of them needs the fixtures moved to a different wall, though a wall-hung vanity may need its waste and water points moved into the wall.
